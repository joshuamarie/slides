What Is a Quadratic Equation?

A quadratic equation is any equation that can be written in the form:

\[ax^2 + bx + c = 0\]

where \(a \neq 0\), and \(a\), \(b\), \(c\) are real numbers.

Quadratic Examples

  • \(x^2 + 5x + 6 = 0\)
  • \(2x^2 - 3x = 0\)
  • \(x^2 - 9 = 0\)

NOT Quadratic

  • \(3x + 5 = 0\) (linear — no \(x^2\))
  • \(x^3 + x^2 = 0\) (cubic — degree 3)
  • \(\frac{1}{x} + 2 = 0\) (rational)

Anatomy of a Quadratic

\[\underbrace{a}_{\text{leads}}x^2 + \underbrace{b}_{\text{middle}}x + \underbrace{c}_{\text{constant}} = 0\]

Identify the values in \(3x^2 - 7x + 2 = 0\):

Part Value
\(a\) \(3\)
\(b\) \(-7\)
\(c\) \(2\)

Key Rule

The coefficient \(a\) must never equal zero. If \(a = 0\), the equation becomes linear, not quadratic.

Method 1: Factoring

Goal: Rewrite \(ax^2 + bx + c\) as a product of two binomials.

Example: Solve \(x^2 + 5x + 6 = 0\)

Step 1 — Find two numbers that multiply to \(c = 6\) and add to \(b = 5\):

\[\text{Factors of 6: } (1,6),\ (2,3) \quad \Rightarrow \quad 2 + 3 = 5\]

Step 2 — Factor the equation:

\[(x + 2)(x + 3) = 0\]

Step 3 — Apply the Zero Product Property:

\[x + 2 = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad x = -2\] \[x + 3 = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad x = -3\]

Solutions: \(x = -2\) or \(x = -3\)

Factoring Practice

Solve by factoring:

\[x^2 - 7x + 12 = 0\]

Hint: Find two numbers that multiply to \(12\) and add to \(-7\).

Answer: \((-3) \times (-4) = 12\) and \((-3) + (-4) = -7\)

\[(x - 3)(x - 4) = 0\]

\[\boxed{x = 3 \quad \text{or} \quad x = 4}\]

Method 2: Quadratic Formula

When factoring is difficult, we use the Quadratic Formula:

\[x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}\]

This formula works for every quadratic equation.

Memory Tip

“Negative b, plus or minus square root, b squared minus 4ac, all over 2a!”

Quadratic Formula — Worked Example

Solve \(2x^2 - 4x - 6 = 0\)

Identify: \[a = 2, \quad b = -4, \quad c = -6\]

Substitute: \[x = \frac{-(-4) \pm \sqrt{(-4)^2 - 4(2)(-6)}}{2(2)}\]

\[x = \frac{4 \pm \sqrt{16 + 48}}{4}\]

\[x = \frac{4 \pm \sqrt{64}}{4} = \frac{4 \pm 8}{4}\]

Two Solutions:

\[x = \frac{4 + 8}{4} = \frac{12}{4} = 3\]

\[x = \frac{4 - 8}{4} = \frac{-4}{4} = -1\]

Tip

\[\boxed{x = 3 \quad \text{or} \quad x = -1}\]

The Discriminant

The part under the radical, \(b^2 - 4ac\), is called the discriminant \((\Delta)\).

\(\Delta > 0\)

Two real solutions

The parabola crosses the x-axis twice.

\(\Delta = 0\)

One real solution

The parabola touches the x-axis exactly once.

\(\Delta < 0\)

No real solutions

The parabola does not cross the x-axis.

The Parabola — Graphing a Quadratic

The graph of \(y = ax^2 + bx + c\) is always a parabola.

Feature Formula
Vertex (turning point) \(x = -\dfrac{b}{2a}\)
Axis of Symmetry \(x = -\dfrac{b}{2a}\)
Opens Up when \(a > 0\)
Opens Down when \(a < 0\)
y-intercept \((0, c)\)
x-intercepts (roots) solutions of the equation

Method 3: Completing the Square

Transform the equation into the form \((x + h)^2 = k\).

Example: Solve \(x^2 + 6x + 5 = 0\)

Step 1 — Move the constant to the right: \[x^2 + 6x = -5\]

Step 2 — Add \(\left(\dfrac{b}{2}\right)^2 = \left(\dfrac{6}{2}\right)^2 = 9\) to both sides: \[x^2 + 6x + 9 = -5 + 9 = 4\]

Step 3 — Write as a perfect square: \[(x + 3)^2 = 4\]

Step 4 — Take square roots: \[x + 3 = \pm 2 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \boxed{x = -1 \quad \text{or} \quad x = -5}\]

Which Method to Use?

Situation Best Method
Easy to factor (small integers) Factoring
\(b = 0\) (e.g. \(x^2 - 16 = 0\)) Square Root
Any quadratic, guaranteed Quadratic Formula
Converting to vertex form Completing the Square

Pro Tip

When in doubt, the Quadratic Formula always works. It is your safety net.

Quick Quiz

What are the solutions of \(x^2 - x - 6 = 0\)?

    1. \(x = 2\) or \(x = -3\)
    1. \(x = -2\) or \(x = 3\)
    1. \(x = 3\) or \(x = -3\)
    1. \(x = 1\) or \(x = -6\)

Answer: B — \(x = -2\) or \(x = 3\)

\((x - 3)(x + 2) = 0 \Rightarrow x = 3\) or \(x = -2\)

What We Learned Today

Key Takeaways

  • A quadratic equation has the form \(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\)
  • We can solve by factoring, quadratic formula, or completing the square
  • The discriminant (\(b^2 - 4ac\)) tells us how many solutions exist
  • The graph of a quadratic is always a parabola
  • Parabolas appear everywhere in the real world
